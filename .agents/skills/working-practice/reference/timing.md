# Numbers, and when they mean anything

A duration is the number most likely to be treated as a fact before it is one. The correction is
not to measure less — precise measurement is where a lot of the quality comes from. It is to
measure the right thing, at the right point, against something real.

## What is fixed before the work starts

Settled once, at the top, and everything is built against them:

- Resolution and aspect ratio
- Frame rate — the number of still pictures shown per second, usually 24 or 30
- Whether there is sound
- The safe area — the margin around the frame that nothing important crosses, so nothing is lost
  on a screen that crops the edges
- The scenes, and the beats inside them — a beat being one thing happening: a word appearing, a
  hand landing, a cut
- The intent: what the video is for and what it says

A frame rate is a fact. A scene list is a decision. Both belong at the head of a document and can
be relied on.

## What stays tentative until the thing is built

- How long each scene runs
- How long the whole video runs
- Where each beat lands inside its scene

Nobody can tell from a description how long a beat needs to be on screen. Early durations exist so
that the parts come out proportionate to one another and can be watched side by side. They are a
starting point and should say so.

Write beat timings as named values at the top of a file rather than as numbers buried in the body,
so adjusting them later means editing a short list rather than searching the file.

## The order that works

1. **Scope roughly.** Put approximate durations on the parts so they are built at comparable
   scale.
2. **Build to those numbers.** Where a part seems to want longer, say so and name the beat and the
   change you think it wants. A silent adjustment is the thing to avoid; the observation is
   useful.
3. **Watch, then measure.** Now the numbers describe something. Write them down with the date.
4. **Add.** Polish, embellishment and decoration land on the finished cut.
5. **Let the length move.** A thirty-second cut becomes thirty-four, or twenty-eight. That is step
   4 working, and it needs no correction.

Step 5 is the one that gets resisted, because a number that drifts looks like a defect. From step
3 onward the number is a description, and descriptions follow the thing they describe.

## Measure against something real

This is the line between measurement that helps and measurement that damages.

**Real** is anything that can be played: a recorded voice, a performance, a take on screen.
Measuring where each word ends in a narration and placing on-screen text on those points is how
type comes to land with speech. Measuring where a performance is genuinely still is how a
slow-down gets aimed at the part that can absorb it. There is no substitute for this work, and it
is worth doing carefully.

**Not real** is a target length, a range, or a budget written in a planning document. Work aimed
at one of those produces edits that serve arithmetic: material cut out of a finished part because
a total ran a few seconds over, or a beat compressed to fit a figure nobody has re-derived since
the parts were built.

When a total comes out over or under, that is information for the person you are working with. It
is not an instruction to cut.

## What a plan's numbers are worth

Any duration written before the parts exist is the author's guess. It is useful for proportion and
for nothing else. Two things follow.

**Do not treat a plan's arithmetic as verified.** Frame budgets, layout coordinates and geometry
written in advance are routinely wrong, and are found wrong by building the thing and looking at
it — not by re-reading the plan.

**Do not defend an estimate against the built thing.** Where a document and the built work
disagree about a duration, the built work is what exists. Report the contradiction and let the
person decide which one gets corrected.

## Transitions take time from both sides

Where two scenes are joined by a transition, the transition usually overlaps both of them — it
consumes frames from the end of the first and from the start of the second. So the joined result
is shorter than the sum of its parts, and a longer transition eats further into its neighbours.

This is worth stating explicitly because the arithmetic looks like a saving and is not: the
missing frames are not compressed, they are scene content that is never shown. A beat placed near
the end of a scene can disappear entirely, while every number in the plan still adds up.

Leave the ends of a scene as holds — nothing arriving, nothing resolving — so a join has somewhere
to happen.

## Reporting

Say what changed and what it does. A per-part table of frame counts describes the work in a
language the decision is not made in, and the person reading it is deciding by watching.

Where a number genuinely is the point — one part is twice as long as its neighbour, the total
moved by ten seconds — say that in a sentence.

## Verification stills

Rendering a single frame as an image is the cheapest way to catch a layout mistake, and worth
doing early and often.

Those images are valid only until the timing changes. A file named after a frame number becomes a
confident, wrong reference as soon as anything is re-timed, so delete them at the end of the pass
that made them rather than leaving them for the next reader.

**A still shows whether the layout is right. It does not show whether the result is any good.** Is
the element inside the safe area, is the shadow on the correct side, do two things overlap, did it
appear at all — those have answers. Whether it works is judged by watching, and that is not yours
to call.
