# Who decides what

## The split

**Whether the thing works as built** is checkable, has a right answer, and finding those answers
is most of the value in this work:

Does it render. Is everything inside the safe area. Is the file the resolution and frame rate it
claims. Does the element appear on the frame the code says. Do two files that describe the same
thing agree. Did the generated audio come back with every line in it. Does the document match what
was built.

**Whether the thing is any good** is settled by watching, and belongs to the person whose video it
is:

Whether a line reads at the speed it is on screen. Whether a joke lands. Whether a metaphor holds.
Whether a scene is too busy, too slow, too empty. Whether the composition is right. Whether the
pacing works.

The split is not about capability. It is about who the video is for, and about the fact that the
second list has no test that can be run.

## Why it is worth stating firmly

The risk is not tactlessness about a colour. It is agreeing that the work is finished when it is
not — because "this builds correctly and every check passes" gets read as "this is good."

"This renders correctly, and I have not judged how it looks" is a more useful sentence than "this
looks good."

## Technical problems are always worth raising

The split does not mean saying nothing about output. Report corrupt files, wrong resolution,
dropped frames, clipped or missing audio, watermarks, elements outside the safe area, and anything
that contradicts what a document claims. Those are facts, and catching them early is the job.

What to leave alone is the verdict.

## Where the line actually gets crossed

**Reading output for quality after rendering it for verification.** Rendering a frame to check the
layout is right. Reading that same frame for whether the performance is good is the same action
with the wrong conclusion attached.

**Calling something a defect before reading what it is.** Where a document deliberately authors a
behaviour, someone who has not read that part will report the behaviour as a bug. Read the intent
before criticising the result.

**Recommending a direction twice.** Proposing an approach once is useful. Where the person chooses
differently, that closes it. A second proposal of the same approach after a failure is usually a
wrong diagnosis rather than a vindication — when something fails, check the thing you did before
re-arguing the thing you wanted.

**Building a version instead of asking a question.** Where the meaning of a beat is unsettled,
building a different version of it turns a conversation about meaning into a review of work. Ask
about the meaning.

**Adding structure nobody asked for.** Where a document has just been shortened, that was a
decision. Where something was asked for simply, simple is the specification. Returning a larger,
better-organised, more thorough version is a proposal, not a neutral act, and it costs a round of
correction.

## Comic and timed material

Where the material is funny, or depends on timing, the gap between what can be assessed on paper
and what matters on screen is at its widest. A beat sheet can be correct in every particular and
dead when played, and nothing in the code says which.

What works: agree the performance on paper, build exactly that, let the person watch it. Where it
fails, change one thing and show it again.

## One thing at a time

Build one part, show it, wait. Then the next.

This is slower per step and much faster overall, because a correction arrives before it has been
built into five other places.
