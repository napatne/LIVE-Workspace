# Two more films

Two films made the same way as the worked example, neither of them in this repository. One
generated people and paid for it. The other generated nothing at all. Between them they are where
most of the rules in this skill came from, and all of it was learned by getting it wrong first.

## Contents

- A film with generated people — what it cost and what it taught
- A film with no assets at all — and the question to ask before generating anything
- Which of the two a given subject wants

---

## A film with generated people

74 seconds. Three animated characters, seven cut-out drawings of people, eight props, a paper
background, nineteen spoken lines. Almost all of it generated, and the only one of three films
that cost money.

**The characters were never photographed.** They came from written descriptions, chosen
deliberately over using pictures of real colleagues.

### Build the frame, do not describe it

Four attempts were made to get a character to come out the right size by describing it, getting
more specific each time, down to exact pixel measurements and *if in doubt make her smaller*. All
four failed.

What worked was assembling the starting picture by hand — the character placed in the frame at the
size and position wanted — and handing that over to be animated. **A generator follows pictures,
not instructions about layout**, however precisely the instructions are written.

This is the rule that saves the most money, and it became the first of fifteen the project kept.

### Four more, each learned expensively

**Never hand over a photograph or someone else's artwork as a style reference.** It gets copied. A
famous cartoon passed in as a mood reference came back reproduced almost exactly. A paper
template contributed its decorative flowers, and separately its watermark.

**Name directions by the screen, not the subject.** *His right* sent a character the wrong way.
*Moves to the left of frame* does not.

**Repeated identical actions merge into one, and rewording does not fix it.** Five nods were asked
for, three times over. About one nod arrived, every time. The finished film still has one, accepted
rather than paid for again.

**Keep the picture that was fed in.** One approved clip can never be remade, because its starting
frame was saved somewhere temporary and lost when the session ended.

### Two things nobody expects

**Video generators cannot write text.** Asked for a title card, one produced convincing-looking
nonsense. Every word in that film was added afterwards in code. Assume this of any generator.

**Every clip came back a slightly different shade of white.** The generated paper background was
not the same colour twice — cut together, the page visibly changed at every join. Fixed by
measuring each clip and correcting it, which is a problem only found by assembling the film and
looking at it.

### The cut-out trick

Seven drawings had to be lifted off their backgrounds. **The obvious method fails:** removing
everything white punches holes through the picture, because parts of the subjects are also white —
a hard hat, a pair of shoes.

What works is flooding in from the edges and removing only white that connects back to the border.
Anything white in the middle, enclosed by the drawing, stays. This is a general problem, not one
film's: cut-outs arriving with holes in them is why.

### What it cost

Three animated clips that shipped, plus **80% again on top, burned on generations nobody used** —
an invented rule that made the movement physically impossible, and a missing line that made the
tool ignore the reference picture entirely.

The free narration tier ran to ten requests per model per day, which sounds generous until one
round of feedback means regenerating all nineteen lines.

### The check that should always run

The narration lost three sentences and nobody noticed. The file was valid, played perfectly, and
was a plausible length — it simply did not contain everything that had been asked for.

**Have generated audio transcribed and compared against the script.** It catches missing lines,
long silences, and the voice reading a stage direction aloud instead of performing it.

## A film with no assets at all

90 seconds, silent, and **no images, video, audio or font files whatsoever.** Not a few — none. On
screen: circles, lines, rectangles and text, drawn by code. Its main character is a coloured dot.
Its progress bar is nine small rectangles. Its only typeface was already on every computer.

Total spend: nothing.

### Why it was right there

**The subject was an idea, not a thing.** A film about judging whether an idea is workable has
nothing to photograph.

**Drawn shapes stay correct.** A generated diagram comes back with the wrong number of things in
it, in the wrong order, with labels that are not quite words. Where the diagram *is* the
explanation, that is not cosmetic.

**Changing your mind is free.** Months later someone reduced five markers to three across four
scenes. With generated images that is a new set of images. Here it was a change to some numbers.

### The rule it gives you

**Before asking for a picture, ask whether the thing wanted is a circle, a line and some words.**
It often is. Code-drawn is exact, re-renders at any size, costs nothing, cannot arrive in the wrong
style, and is edited by changing a number.

## Which of the two a given subject wants

The test is not whether something *can* be drawn — almost anything can. It is **whether being exact
matters more than being rich.**

| Draw it in code | Generate it |
| --- | --- |
| Diagrams, processes, comparisons | People, places |
| Anything with labelled parts | Atmosphere, texture |
| Anything whose accuracy is the point | Anything whose warmth is the point |

No amount of shape-drawing introduces three human beings warmly. That film spent roughly ten
dollars and it was the right decision.

---

*Costs and free-tier limits above were true when the work was done, through mid-2026. Provider
pricing and quotas age faster than anything else here — re-check before relying on a number.*
