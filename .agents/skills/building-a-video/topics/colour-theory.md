## hook · 6s · core

**Figure:** wheel — all twelve hues, nothing marked

Colour theory is how colours work together — and most of it lives on one wheel.

## wheel · 10s · core

**Figure:** wheel — primary red, yellow, blue marked; secondary orange, green, violet marked lighter

Three primaries — red, yellow, blue. Mix two and you get a secondary: orange, green, violet.
Mix further and you get the six tertiaries.

## complementary · 8s · core

**Figure:** wheel — red and green opposite each other, both lit, the mixed result shown between

Opposite colours are complementary — red and green, blue and orange. Side by side they
intensify each other. Mixed, they dull.

## properties · 11s · standard

**Figure:** ramp — one hue across value, then the same hue across saturation

Every colour has three properties. Hue is which colour it is. Value is how light or dark.
Saturation is how intense — how far it sits from grey.

## analogous · 9s · standard

**Figure:** wheel — blue, blue-green and green lit, adjacent

Three neighbours on the wheel are analogous — blue, blue-green, green. They agree with each
other, so the result feels calm.

## warm-cool · 10s · standard

**Figure:** wheel — the warm half and the cool half divided and labelled

The wheel splits in half. Reds, oranges and yellows read as warm and energetic. Blues, greens
and violets read as cool and calm.

## origins · 12s · extended

**Figure:** flow — Forsius, early 1600s → Newton's prism → Opticks, 1704

The first colour wheel was drawn by Aron Sigfrid Forsius in the early 1600s. Newton built the
modern one after splitting sunlight with a prism, published in Opticks in 1704.

## triadic · 10s · extended

**Figure:** wheel — three hues lit, evenly spaced

Three colours evenly spaced around the wheel are triadic. Strong contrast, still balanced —
but let one lead and keep the other two quiet.

## split-complementary · 11s · extended

**Figure:** wheel — a base hue lit, plus the two either side of its opposite

Split-complementary takes a colour and, instead of its opposite, the two colours either side
of that opposite. You keep the contrast and lose some of the tension.

## monochromatic · 9s · extended

**Figure:** ramp — one hue only, tints through shades

Monochromatic uses one hue only — its tints and shades. Nothing clashes, because there is
nothing to clash with.

## tetradic · 10s · extended

**Figure:** wheel — four hues lit forming a rectangle, then squared

Tetradic uses four colours — two complementary pairs, forming a rectangle. Space them evenly
into a square and you get the square scheme.

## practice · 8s · extended

**Figure:** bars — Contrast 40, Meaning 35, Consistency 25

Harmony is not enough on its own. Check contrast so text stays readable — colour carries
meaning before it carries style.

## recap · 3s · core

**Figure:** wheel — all twelve hues, nothing marked

One wheel — everything else is position on it.
